PTSB Takeover Clears a Hurdle, Central Bank Flags More Unauthorised Lenders

PTSBCentral Bank of IrelandBudget 2027Consumer ProtectionPersonal Loans

PTSB-BAWAG deal survives a challenge

The big story for Irish borrowers this week is the progress of the €1.6 billion acquisition of PTSB by Austrian group BAWAG. The Irish Takeover Panel has rejected a challenge to the deal, clearing one of the obstacles standing in the way of completion. Separately, the Irish Times reports that the panel also dismissed complaints brought by former Bank of Scotland (Ireland) chief Mark Duffy, who is said to be weighing up a late bid of his own for the lender.

For existing PTSB customers and anyone with a mortgage, loan or savings account there, this is a deal worth watching rather than worrying about. Takeovers of this scale typically unfold over many months, and changes to products, rates or terms don't happen overnight. Still, if the sale does proceed, it's a reminder to keep an eye on any correspondence from your lender about what — if anything — changes for existing customers.

More unauthorised firms flagged by the Central Bank

The Central Bank of Ireland issued a fresh batch of warnings this week, naming Trusted Loan Capital, McFall Capital, Prestige Credit Limited and Trust Line Fund as unauthorised firms. None of these are regulated to provide lending or credit services in Ireland, and consumers dealing with any of them have none of the protections that come with an authorised lender.

This is now a near-weekly occurrence, and it's worth repeating the basics: before signing up for any loan, credit line or investment product, check the firm against the Central Bank's register of warnings and its list of authorised providers. If a lender is pushing hard for quick decisions, upfront fees, or contact only through messaging apps or unofficial channels, treat that as a red flag regardless of how professional the website looks.

Budget 2027 and the squeeze on everyday finances

On the household finances side, the ESRI says changes to direct taxes, welfare and childcare supports in this week's Budget will deliver "small income gains" for most people. It's a modest rather than transformative change, and with living costs still a daily pressure point for many households, those gains are likely to be absorbed quickly rather than freeing up much extra breathing room.

In a lighter but telling piece of research, AIB found that social awkwardness around splitting bills and chasing small debts is costing people an estimated €150 a year. It's a small number in isolation, but it's a useful nudge: little leaks in day-to-day money management — uncollected IOUs, overpaid shared bills, forgotten subscriptions — add up over a year, and are worth tracking alongside bigger-ticket items like loan repayments or credit card balances.

What this means if you're borrowing or saving

Nothing this week points to an imminent change in the direction of interest rates, but the ECB's policy stance remains the key variable to watch for anyone with a variable-rate mortgage, a personal loan, or money sitting in savings. If you're shopping around for a personal loan or credit card, it's still worth comparing offers across several lenders rather than defaulting to your current bank, and always checking a firm's authorisation status first.

This is general commentary, not financial advice. Always confirm current rates and terms directly with a lender before applying.
