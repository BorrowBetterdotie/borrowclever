Budget 2027: What the Tax Cuts, Welfare Boosts and Savings Changes Mean for Your Wallet

Budget 2027TaxSavingsHelp-to-BuyECB

Budget 2027 delivers a mixed bag for households

Budget Day has come and gone, and Budget 2027 has turned out to be a genuine giveaway budget, with the Government confirming an overall package of roughly €8.5 billion in additional spending, including around €1.3 billion in tax cuts. For anyone trying to plan household finances, there's a lot to digest.

Tax and income changes

The headline measure for workers is a set of income tax adjustments that the Government says will benefit middle-income earners by up to €1,500 a year. The minimum wage is also rising by 79 cent, while the inheritance tax-free threshold for transfers from parents to children is expected to increase by €20,000. Separately, the Government confirmed it is cutting the standard capital gains tax rate to 31 per cent, alongside enhancements to the R&D tax credit scheme that include a 20 per cent increase in first-year refunds.

Savings, Help-to-Buy and other new measures

One of the more notable announcements for savers is a new scheme with a €50,000 tax-free threshold, alongside a separate €12,000-per-year limit for an investment scheme. The Help-to-Buy grant is being increased by €5,000, which should be welcome news for first-time buyers navigating what remains a tight property market — though recent Eurostat figures suggest Irish house price inflation has slowed compared with much of the euro zone.

On the welfare side, there's a €10 increase in weekly social welfare rates and a €5 rise in the fuel allowance, while the third-level student contribution fee is being permanently cut by €150. Self-employed childminders also benefit from an increased tax exemption under Childcare Services Relief, and farm succession partnerships will see their tax credit doubled.

Meanwhile, savings rates and bank ownership are moving too

Away from the budget, Revolut has increased the interest rate on balances up to €2,500 in its main savings account, giving some savers a modest boost regardless of budget measures. Separately, speculation continues around a possible bid for PTSB, with Mark Duffy's last-minute approach adding uncertainty to the picture just as budget ministers try to finalise their plans. On the inflation front, ECB chief economist Philip Lane has said the euro zone is not yet seeing strong second-round inflation effects from high energy costs, though he cautioned the extent of any future pass-through remains uncertain.

For borrowers, the combination of tax changes, a new savings vehicle, and ongoing uncertainty around interest rate direction means it's worth reviewing your own numbers rather than relying on headline figures alone.
