Representative APR Explained: How Irish Credit Card Rates Work

The €1,500 / 12-month formula behind every Irish card's headline rate · Rates referenced verified 22 July 2026

Every Irish credit card advertises a "representative APR" — currently ranging from 13.8% to 22.9% across the market. But few borrowers know what the number actually represents, why it never matches the purchase rate on the same card, or when it should drive their decision. This guide decodes the metric our credit card comparison ranks by.

The definition: a standardised scenario, set by law

Every Irish credit card issuer is legally required by the CCPC to publish a representative APR. The figure is built on a standardised scenario:

  • a €1,500 balance,
  • repaid over 12 months,
  • including the mandatory €30 government stamp duty that applies to every Irish credit card each year.

Because every issuer must use the same scenario, the representative APR is the one number that lets you compare cards like-for-like. A personal loan can be ranked by total cost of credit because it has a fixed amount and term; a credit card has neither, so the representative APR is the closest thing to a standardised "price tag" the card market has.

Why the APR is always higher than the purchase rate

Look at any card and you'll see two rates. AIB Click, the cheapest card in the Irish market, charges a 9.11% purchase rate but shows a 13.8% representative APR. Neither is wrong — they measure different things:

  • The purchase rate is the nominal interest rate applied to your spending.
  • The representative APR wraps that interest plus the €30 stamp duty into one standardised annual figure on the €1,500 scenario.

On a €1,500 balance, a fixed €30 duty alone adds roughly two percentage points — which is why the gap between purchase rate and APR is biggest on the cheapest cards. Compare cards on the APR, not the purchase rate: it's the figure that includes the unavoidable cost.

What the representative APR does not tell you

It's not your personal rate

It's a standardised scenario. Your actual cost depends on your balance, your repayment pattern, and your card's specific terms.

It excludes introductory offers

0% balance-transfer and purchase offers sit outside the APR. An Post Money Classic has a 22.9% APR and Ireland's longest 0% balance-transfer window (12 months). For a disciplined balance transfer, the intro offer matters more than the APR — see how balance transfers work →

It doesn't capture annual card fees beyond stamp duty

Most Irish cards charge no fee beyond the €30 stamp duty, but Bank of Ireland Platinum Advantage (~€76.18/year) and the BOI Aer Credit Card (~€78/year) do. Weigh those separately against any rewards.

It's irrelevant if you clear your balance monthly

Pay your full balance every month and you pay zero interest, regardless of the APR. For disciplined payers, rewards and fees decide the best card; the APR ranking is the right metric only for anyone who expects to carry a balance.

How to actually use the number

  1. Expect to carry a balance? Rank by representative APR — lowest wins. That's exactly how our comparison table is ordered.
  2. Moving existing debt? Look at balance-transfer intro offers first, and check the revert APR for whatever balance might remain — start with the current best balance-transfer offers →
  3. Clear your balance every month? The APR barely matters; compare fees and rewards instead.
