Debt Consolidation Loans in Ireland: When They Make Sense (2026)

The rate maths, the balance-transfer alternative, and the term trap · Rates referenced verified 22 July 2026

Debt consolidation means taking one new loan to clear several existing debts — typically credit cards, overdrafts, or older loans — so you're left with a single repayment at (ideally) a much lower rate. In Ireland the arithmetic can be compelling: standard credit card APRs run up to 22.9%, while standard personal loans start at 7.20% APR. But consolidation only saves money under specific conditions, and there's a cheaper alternative for some situations. This guide covers both.

The core maths: card rates vs loan rates

Suppose you're carrying €5,000 of credit card debt at a standard card rate and would realistically take 3 years to clear it. Compare the interest bill at card rates versus personal loan rates (calculated with the standard compound interest formula, rounded):

Debt held as APR Monthly (approx.) Interest over 3 years (approx.)
Credit card balance (standard rate) 22.9% €187.86 €1,763
Personal loan (AIB standard) 8.95% €158.08 €691
Personal loan (PTSB, best standard rate) 7.20% €154.32 €556

Moving €5,000 of card debt into the cheapest standard personal loan saves roughly €1,200 in interest over three years — and swaps an open-ended minimum-payment cycle for a fixed schedule with a guaranteed end date. That fixed end date is half the value of consolidation: the debt is structurally forced to shrink.

The alternative: a 0% balance transfer

If your debt is credit card debt and the balance is small enough to clear within 6–12 months, a 0% balance transfer can beat any consolidation loan — zero interest beats 7.20%. Irish 0% windows currently run from 6 months (Revolut, PTSB Ice) up to 12 months (An Post Money Classic, Ireland's longest).

The decision comes down to size and speed:

  • Balance transfer wins when the balance can realistically be cleared inside the 0% window, and the debt is on cards (transfers must come from a different issuer's card).
  • Consolidation loan wins for larger balances, mixed debt types (cards plus overdraft plus an old loan), or anything that needs several years — because when a 0% window closes, card rates revert to 16.8%–22.9% APR.

Where to get a consolidation loan

Any Irish personal loan can be used to consolidate. The market's standard rates (verified 22 July 2026, full table on the loans comparison page →):

  • PTSB — 7.20% APR, €1,000–€75,000, up to 84 months. The best standard rate in the market.
  • Bank of Ireland — 8.30% APR, €2,000–€75,000, with the option to defer your first 3 repayments.
  • An Post Money — 8.40% APR fixed (6.90% APR for €20,000+), no early repayment fee, terms up to 120 months.
  • Revolut — 8.41% APR average (6.5%–12.99% by credit score), €1,000–€50,000, app-only.
  • AIB — 8.95% APR, €1,000–€75,000, 3-hour decision for existing customers.

Credit unions are also an option if you're a member: the ILCU average is 10.42% APR — above the best bank rates — but individual credit unions vary widely, and your savings record with them counts in your favour. See credit union vs bank loans →

The term trap: don't turn a saving into a cost

Consolidation's biggest pitfall is stretching the new loan out to make the monthly payment look good. A longer term at a lower rate can still cost more in total than the debt you consolidated. At Bank of Ireland's 8.30% APR, €10,000 costs roughly €1,281 in interest over 3 years — but €3,092 over 7 years. The rate didn't change; the term more than doubled the price.

Pick the shortest term whose repayment you can genuinely sustain, and check the early-repayment terms so you can clear it faster if your situation improves. The full mechanics are in Total cost of credit explained →

The discipline condition

Consolidation clears your cards; it doesn't close them. The saving above assumes the cleared cards stay at zero. If new balances build up alongside the consolidation loan, you end up servicing both — the one outcome worse than where you started. If you consolidate card debt, decide up front what happens to the cards: keep one for genuine emergencies if you must, and treat the rest as done.

A quick decision checklist

  1. Total up your debts and their rates. Consolidation helps most when you're carrying balances at card-level rates (16.8%–22.9%).
  2. Small card balance, clearable within a year? A 0% balance transfer is likely cheaper than any loan.
  3. Larger or mixed debt? Compare loan APRs on the comparison table and pick the shortest sustainable term.
  4. Credit union member? Get their quote too before deciding.
  5. Commit to not rebuilding the card balances. Otherwise the maths collapses.
