Ireland's New Investment Account Explained (Budget 2027)

The €50,000 tax-free threshold, the 1% charge and the €12,000 annual cap, with worked examples · Based on Budget 2027 announcements of 6 October 2026

Budget 2027 confirmed that Ireland will get a new tax-advantaged investment account from 1 July 2027. It's being called the Irish Investment Account, and in coverage you'll also see it called the Savings and Investment Account (SIA) or Personal Investment Account. The Government wants to move some of the roughly €170 billion sitting in low-interest Irish deposit accounts into long-term investments. This guide covers what has been announced, how the tax works, and who it suits.

The account still has to be legislated for in the Finance Bill, and the detailed rules can change before it launches. We'll update this guide when the legislation is published.

The headline rules

Feature What's been announced
Opens1 July 2027
Who can open oneIrish tax-resident adults (18+) with a PPSN, one account per person
Contribution limit€12,000 a year (reported as €1,000 a month), with no minimum
Tax-free thresholdThe first €50,000 of account value
Tax above the thresholdA flat 1% a year on the account value above €50,000
Taxes that won't apply insideCapital gains tax, dividend withholding tax, exit tax (investment undertaking tax), life assurance exit tax, and the 8-year deemed disposal rule
What you can holdListed shares, listed bonds, instruments traded on regulated markets, and retail investment funds including ETFs
What you can't holdCrypto and derivatives. Cash is only allowed briefly around buying and selling.
Who offers itBanks, investment firms and insurers
PaperworkThe provider calculates the tax and pays it to Revenue. You don't file anything for normal running of the account.

How the 1% tax actually works

This part is most often misunderstood. The 1% is not charged on your profits. It's charged each year on the value of the account above €50,000, which is similar to how Sweden's ISK account is taxed.

  • Account worth €40,000: no tax.
  • Account worth €52,000: 1% of €2,000, which is €20.
  • Account worth €80,000: 1% of €30,000, which is €300.

That cuts both ways. In a good year, 1% of the value is much less than the tax you'd normally pay on the gain. In a bad year you still pay it. If an €80,000 account falls 10% to €72,000, you'd still owe about €220, even though you lost money. Industry critics raised this on Budget day.

The €12,000 cap means nobody pays any tax at first. Even putting in the maximum every year, you'd need around four years of contributions and growth to pass €50,000.

Worked example: €12,000 a year for 10 years

Suppose you contribute the full €12,000 each year for 10 years (€120,000 in total) and the investments grow by 5% a year. Here's how three options compare. These are illustrations, not forecasts.

Where the money goes Tax treatment Value after 10 years
Irish Investment Account 1% a year on value above €50k (about €3,700 in total) ≈ €154,400
Same ETF held outside the account 38% exit tax on the €38,500 gain ≈ €143,900
Deposit account at 2.5% AER 33% DIRT on interest ≈ €131,600

In this example the account comes out about €10,500 ahead of holding the same fund outside it. That's before counting the hassle it avoids: no 8-year deemed disposal bills and no self-assessment returns. The ETF figure is generous to the outside option because it ignores deemed disposal, which would bring part of that tax bill forward.

The deposit row is the comparison the Government is targeting, but it isn't like-for-like. A deposit doesn't fall in value and an investment can. Higher expected returns come with the risk of losses, especially over short periods.

What else changed for investors in Budget 2027

Exit tax on funds and ETFs held outside the account

Exit tax was cut from 41% to 38% in Budget 2026. Law firm McCann FitzGerald's Budget 2027 briefing reports a further cut to 35% from 1 January 2027 for investment undertaking tax and life assurance exit tax. Treat that as provisional until the Finance Bill confirms it.

Deemed disposal

The 8-year deemed disposal rule won't apply inside the new account. For funds held outside it, Budget 2027 coverage doesn't point to any reform. Deemed disposal still applies to ETFs and funds in an ordinary brokerage account.

Who the account suits, and who should wait

Likely to suit

  • People with savings well beyond an emergency fund that they won't need for 5+ years.
  • Anyone who has avoided ETFs because of deemed disposal and exit-tax paperwork.
  • Regular monthly investors. The €1,000/month limit matches a standing-order approach.

Think twice first

  • You're carrying expensive debt. See the next section.
  • You need the money within a few years. For a house deposit or car, the risk of a market fall matters more than the tax saving.
  • You haven't used your pension tax relief. Tax advisers quoted on Budget day pointed out that for many people, pension contributions with income tax relief at 20–40% will still beat this account. It works best as the next step once pension relief is used.

Pay off expensive debt before you invest

Repaying debt gives you a guaranteed, tax-free return equal to its interest rate. Irish credit cards currently charge 13.8% to 22.9% representative APR, and standard personal loans run from about 7% to 10.5% APR. Long-term stock market returns have historically been in the mid-single digits after inflation, and they aren't guaranteed. Clearing a 20% credit card balance will almost always beat investing the same money.

If you have debts, a sensible order is:

  1. Build a cash emergency fund, ideally 3–6 months of essential spending, in the best-paying deposit account you can find.
  2. Clear high-interest debt. Look at a 0% balance transfer or a cheaper consolidation loan to cut the cost while you do it.
  3. Use any pension tax relief you're entitled to.
  4. Then consider the Irish Investment Account for long-term money from July 2027.

Still to be confirmed

The Budget announcements set out the main rules, but several practical questions are still open until the Finance Bill and provider terms are published:

  • Whether withdrawals are restricted, and whether money you take out frees up contribution room again.
  • Whether you can move your account from one provider to another.
  • What happens to the account on death, and how it interacts with inheritance tax.
  • What fees providers will charge. Platform and fund fees could easily cost more than the 1% tax for smaller accounts.
  • Which specific funds and ETFs will be available on each platform.
