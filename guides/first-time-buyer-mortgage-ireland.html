First-Time Buyer Mortgage Guide Ireland (2026): Deposit to Drawdown

The borrowing limits, the deposit, the government schemes, and every step of the process — explained without the jargon

A mortgage is the one loan where the rules matter as much as the rate. Before any Irish lender looks at your application, the Central Bank's mortgage measures decide how much you can borrow and how much deposit you need — and a set of government schemes can materially change what you can afford. This guide walks a first-time buyer through the whole sequence: the limits, the deposit, the schemes, the approval process, and the costs nobody mentions until the week before closing.

A note on rates: BorrowClever doesn't yet track mortgage rates the way we track personal loans and credit cards, so this guide deliberately avoids quoting individual lenders' mortgage pricing. Where we show repayment figures, they're illustrations at a clearly-labelled example rate. For live mortgage rate comparisons, use the CCPC's independent comparison tool at ccpc.ie.

Step 0: how much you're allowed to borrow

The Central Bank of Ireland's mortgage measures set two limits for first-time buyers:

  • Loan-to-income (LTI): you can borrow up to 4× your gross annual income (combined income for joint applicants).
  • Loan-to-value (LTV): you can borrow up to 90% of the property's value — meaning a minimum 10% deposit.

Worked example: a couple earning €80,000 combined can borrow up to €320,000. At 90% LTV that supports a purchase price of roughly €355,500, with a deposit of about €35,500.

Lenders can exceed these limits for a small share of their annual lending, so an "exemption" is possible — typically for strong earners with clean finances — but it's discretionary, capped, and often exhausted late in the calendar year. Plan on the standard limits and treat an exemption as a bonus.

Within the Central Bank's ceilings, each lender then applies its own affordability test: they'll want to see you can sustain repayments at a rate higher than today's (a stress test), evidenced by your current rent plus regular savings.

Step 1: the deposit — and where it's allowed to come from

  • Your savings. Lenders want 6+ months of consistent saving. A €35,000 deposit that appeared last Tuesday raises questions; €600 a month for four years answers them.
  • A family gift. Permitted by all mainstream lenders, with a signed gift letter confirming the money is not repayable and the giver has no stake in the property.
  • Help to Buy. For new builds only, the tax rebate below can fund a large slice of the deposit — up to €30,000.

Just as important as the deposit itself is account conduct. In the six months before applying: no missed payments, no gambling patterns, no unexplained overdrafts. Lenders read your statements the way an examiner reads an exam script.

Step 2: the government schemes that change the maths

Help to Buy (HTB)

A Revenue rebate of income tax and DIRT paid over the previous four years, worth up to the lesser of €30,000 or 10% of the purchase price, on new builds and self-builds priced up to €500,000. Conditions: you must be a first-time buyer, take a mortgage of at least 70% LTV, and live in the home for five years — selling earlier can trigger a clawback. Apply through Revenue's myAccount before you need the funds; approval takes time.

First Home Scheme (FHS)

A shared-equity scheme: the State and participating banks bridge the gap between your mortgage + deposit and the price of a new-build home, taking an equity stake of up to 30% (up to 20% if combined with Help to Buy). Regional price ceilings apply, and a service charge on the equity kicks in after the early years — the stake is a real cost, not free money, and you can buy it back over time.

Local Authority Home Loan

A State-backed mortgage for buyers within income limits who can show two mortgage refusals (or insufficient offers) from commercial lenders. Worth knowing about as a fallback if bank approval falls short.

Step 3: approval in principle

Approval in Principle (AIP) is a lender's statement of how much it would lend you, based on your documents but before you've found a property. It's what estate agents ask for before taking a bid seriously, and it's typically valid for 6–12 months depending on the lender.

The document checklist is broadly the same everywhere:

  • 6 months of current account and savings statements
  • Payslips (typically 3–6 months) and a salary certificate or employment detail summary
  • Photo ID and proof of address
  • For the self-employed: 2–3 years of accounts and tax confirmations

AIP is not a guarantee — the formal loan offer comes later and depends on the property valuation and final underwriting. Don't waive contingencies on the strength of AIP alone.

Step 4: from sale agreed to drawdown

  1. Valuation. The lender instructs a valuer (you pay the fee) to confirm the property supports the loan.
  2. Formal loan offer. The binding version of your approval, checked by your solicitor.
  3. Survey (optional but wise). The lender's valuation is not a structural survey. An independent surveyor's report is your protection against buying someone else's problem.
  4. Mortgage protection insurance. Required by law before drawdown in almost all cases — a life policy that clears the mortgage if you die. You can (and usually should) shop for it separately rather than taking your lender's policy.
  5. Home insurance. Also required before drawdown.
  6. Contracts and closing. Your solicitor exchanges contracts, requests the funds, and the lender draws down the mortgage to complete the purchase.

Choosing the mortgage itself: rate type, term, and the cashback trap

Fixed vs variable

Most Irish first-time buyers now fix for an initial period (commonly 3–5 years, with longer fixes available). Fixed gives certainty; variable gives flexibility to overpay without penalty. Many lenders also price green mortgages (better rates for BER A/B homes) below their standard book — the same pattern as the green personal loans on our loans page.

Compare on APRC, not the headline rate

The APRC (annual percentage rate of charge) is the mortgage market's all-in comparison figure — it does for mortgages what APR does for personal loans and representative APR does for credit cards: it folds the initial rate, the roll-off rate and the standard fees into one number across the full term.

Cashback is not a discount

Cashback offers (commonly 1–2% of the loan) are marketing, funded by the rate. On a €320,000 mortgage, a rate difference of a fraction of a percent costs far more over the term than any upfront cashback pays you. Compare APRC first; treat cashback as a tiebreaker.

The term decides the total cost as much as the rate

Illustration at an example rate of 4.0% on a €300,000 mortgage (standard annuity calculation, rounded — not a quote):

Term Monthly (approx.) Total interest (approx.)
25 years €1,584 €175,000
30 years €1,432 €216,000
35 years €1,328 €258,000

Stretching from 25 to 35 years cuts the monthly payment by about €256 — and adds roughly €83,000 in interest. The same term trap we cover for personal loans in Total cost of credit explained → applies here at ten times the scale. Take the longer term only if you need it to qualify, and overpay when you can.

The costs beyond the deposit

  • Stamp duty — 1% of the purchase price at typical first-time buyer price levels (higher bands apply above €1m).
  • Legal fees — conveyancing plus outlays; get quotes from two or three solicitors.
  • Valuation fee — payable to the lender's valuer.
  • Survey fee — optional but recommended, especially for second-hand homes.
  • Insurance — mortgage protection and home insurance, both in place before drawdown.

Budget a few thousand euro beyond the deposit so closing costs don't eat the savings buffer you'll want as a new homeowner.

The sequence, compressed

  1. Clean up account conduct; build the savings record.
  2. Work out your ceiling: 4× income, 10% deposit, plus HTB/FHS if buying new.
  3. Apply for Help to Buy early if eligible (new builds).
  4. Get Approval in Principle — from more than one lender if you can; it's also your negotiating position.
  5. House-hunt inside your real budget, including closing costs.
  6. Sale agreed → valuation → loan offer → survey → insurance → contracts → drawdown.
